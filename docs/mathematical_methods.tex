\section{Solving the PDE}

We begin by writing down the problem explicitly, noting that the diffusion coefficient $D(t) = D(V(t))$ is time-dependent due to the voltage sweep:

\begin{equation}
    \begin{cases}
        u_t = D(t)u_{xx}\\
        u(0,t) = f(t)\\
        u_x(L,t) = 0\\
        u(x,0) = u(x,k\tilde{T})
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

We can solve this equation by shifting the inhomogeneity from the boundary into the main differential equation. Let $u(x,t) = v(x,t) + f(t)$:

\begin{equation}
    \label{v PDE}
    \begin{cases}
        v_t = D(t)v_{xx} - f'(t)\\
        v(0,t) = 0\\
        v_x(L,t) = 0\\
        v(x,0) + f(0) = v(x,k\tilde{T}) + f(k\tilde{T})
    \end{cases}
\end{equation}

We will approach this with the Spectral Method. We write:

\begin{equation}
    v(x,t) = \sum_{n=1}^\infty T_n(t)\phi_n(x)
\end{equation}

To match our boundary conditions, we conveniently choose the basis function $\phi_n(x) = \sin(\lambda_n x)$ with $\lambda_n = \frac{(2n-1)\pi}{2L}$. Putting this solution back into Eq.~\ref{v PDE}, we will solve for the $T_n(t)$ to obtain the full solution.

\begin{equation}
    \label{not projected}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty T_n(t)\sin(\lambda_n x)\right) = D(t)\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty T_n(t)\sin(\lambda_nx)\right) - f'(t)
\end{equation}

Next, we project $f'(t)$ onto our basis functions. Although $f'(t)$ is a constant in $x$, and is therefore even, we can perform an odd extension of our function and focus only on the interval of interest, $[0,L]$. This allows us to write:

\begin{equation}
    f'(t) = f'(t)\sum_{n=1}^\infty c_n \sin(\lambda_n x)
\end{equation}
where the coefficients are given by:

\begin{equation}
    c_n = \frac{2}{L}\int_0^L \sin(\lambda_n x)\;dx
\end{equation}

Substituting this back, we rewrite Eq.~\ref{not projected} as:

\begin{equation}
    \frac{\partial}{\partial t}\left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty T_n(t)\sin(\lambda_n x)\right) = D(t)\frac{\partial^2}{\partial x^2}\left(\sum_{n=1}^\infty T_n(t)\sin(\lambda_nx)\right) - \sum_{n=1}^\infty f'(t)c_n \sin(\lambda_n x)
\end{equation}

Multiplying both sides of the equation by $\sin(\lambda_m x)$ and integrating from $0$ to $L$, we utilize the following orthogonality relationship:

\begin{equation}
    \int_0^L\sin(\lambda_n x)\sin(\lambda_m x)\; dx= \frac{L}{2}\delta_{mn}
\end{equation}

This trick gives us the following equation for each $m$:
\begin{equation}
    T'_m(t) = -D(t)\lambda_m^2T_m - f'(t)c_m
\end{equation}

We solve this by using an integrating factor:
\begin{equation}
    \frac{d}{dt}\left( T_m(t)\exp\left(\lambda_m^2\int_0^t D(s)\; ds\right)\right) = -f'(t)c_m\exp\left(\lambda_m^2\int_0^t D(s)\; ds\right)
\end{equation}

We integrate over each individual time step $[t_{i-1},t_i]$ and define $\Delta t_i = t_i - t_{i-1}$. Assuming the time step is small enough that $D(t) \approx D(t_i)$ is roughly constant over this interval, we obtain:

\begin{equation}
    T_m(t_i) = T_m(t_{i-1})e^{-D(t_i)\lambda_m^2\Delta t_i} - c_m\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}f'(\tau)e^{-D(t_i)\lambda_m^2(t_i-\tau)}\; d\tau
\end{equation}

Assuming $\Delta t_i$ is sufficiently small, the exponential inside the integral is approximately one for $\tau\in [t_{i-1},t_i]$. This yields:

\begin{align*}
        T_m(t_i) \approx & T_m(t_{i-1})e^{-D(t_i)\lambda_m^2\Delta t_i} - c_m\int_{t_{i-1}}^{t_i}f'(\tau)\; d\tau\\
        =& T_m(t_{i-1})e^{-D(t_i)\lambda_m^2\Delta t_i} + c_m\left[f(t_{i-1}) - f(t_i)\right]
\end{align*}

Evaluating this iteratively for $i = 1,2,\dots$, we identify a pattern and can explicitly write:

\begin{equation}
    T_m(t_N) = T_m(t_0)\left(\prod_{i = 1}^{N} e^{-D(t_i)\lambda_m^2\Delta t_i}\right) + \sum_{i = 1}^N\left(c_m[f(t_{i-1}) - f(t_i)]\prod_{j = i+1}^N e^{-D(t_j)\lambda_m^2\Delta t_j}\right)
\end{equation}

Combining this with the periodic condition $T_m(0) = T_m(k\tilde{T})$, we can rewrite the expression to solve for the starting condition:

\begin{equation}
\label{Init Time}
    T_m(0) = \frac{\sum_{i = 1}^N\left(c_m[f(t_{i-1}) - f(t_i)]\prod_{j = i+1}^N e^{-D(t_j)\lambda_m^2\Delta t_j}\right)}{1-\left(\prod_{i = 1}^{N} e^{-D(t_i)\lambda_m^2\Delta t_i}\right) }
\end{equation}

With the initial periodic state $T_m(0)$, we can evaluate $T_m(t_i)$ for all time steps. Substituting these coefficients yields the solution to the problem for $v(x,t)$. Finally, by adding the boundary function $f(t)$ back, we recover the complete theoretical solution to the original partial differential equation:

\begin{equation}
    u(x,t) = f(t) + \sum_{m=1}^\infty T_m(t)\sin(\lambda_m x)
\end{equation}

\section{Numerical Implementation}

To map the mathematical solution to experimental Cyclic Voltammetry (CV) data, the model was implemented using Python. We utilize the JAX library for Just-In-Time (JIT) compilation and automatic differentiation to solve the inverse problem.

\subsection{Modeling the Boundary Condition and Density of States}

The time-dependent boundary condition $f(t)$ represents the equilibrium surface concentration driven by $V(t)$. This is determined by integrating the Density of States (DOS) up to the current voltage:
\begin{equation}
    f(V) = \int_{-\infty}^{V} \text{DOS}(v)\; dv
\end{equation}
To capture the complex redox behavior of the system, we model the true DOS as a superposition of $P$ sub-bands. The exact analytical integral of a Gaussian yields an Error Function ($\text{erf}$). However, evaluating the Error Function iteratively is computationally expensive. To bypass this, we approximate the integrated peaks using a sum of logistic functions. We parameterize the boundary condition as:
\begin{equation}
    f(V) = \sum_{p=1}^P \frac{w_p}{1 + \exp\left[-\gamma(V - V_p)\right]}
\end{equation}
where $w_p$ is the weight (capacity) of sub-band $p$, $V_p$ is its centre, and $\gamma$ is the sharpness shared by all of them. This formulation allows JAX to rapidly compute both $f(t)$ and the DOS analytically without relying on expensive numerical integration.

Only the weights are free. The centres $V_p$ are fixed on a uniform grid spanning the measured potential window, and the single shared $\gamma$ is held at $F/RT$ at 298 K, which is the sharpest a one-electron site can physically be. Letting the centres and the widths float as well makes the recovered DOS non-unique, since neighbouring sub-bands collapse onto one another; fitted freely, $\gamma$ ran to the narrowest the grid allowed at every $P$ we tried, which is the mark of a knob trading against the weights rather than a measurement of site broadening. Fixing them has a second benefit: the simulated current is then exactly linear in the $w_p$, which is what makes recovering them well behaved.

\subsection{Forward Model and Time-Stepping}

To model the time-dependent diffusion coefficient, we use a phenomenological Gaussian distribution around a central voltage $V_c$, with asymmetric rates $\beta_L$ and $\beta_R$ applying below and above $V_c$ respectively:
\begin{equation}
    D(V) = D_0 \exp\left[\beta_{L,R} (V - V_c)^2\right], \qquad \beta_{L,R} \geq 0.
\end{equation}
Keeping the exponents non-negative holds the minimum of $D(V)$ at $V_c$, and we confine $V_c$ to the interior of the measured window. If the fit returns both exponents at zero then $D(V)$ is flat and $V_c$ is no longer determined by anything, so we withhold it rather than report it as a fitted value.

The periodic initial condition is computed directly using Eq.~\ref{Init Time}. This recursion is calculated rapidly using \texttt{jax.lax.scan}, which accumulates the exponential decay and forcing matrices. 

A single diffusivity cannot reconcile scans taken at different sweep rates. Fitted one at a time, each scan is described well but demands its own $D$, rising roughly as the square root of the sweep rate, so the joint residual grows with the ratio of sweep rates rather than with any individual scan. This is what a distribution of transport timescales looks like, and the coarsest representation of one is two environments: a fraction $f$ of the sites where transport is fast, and the remainder where it is slower by a factor $r$,
\begin{equation}
    I = f\, I(D) + (1-f)\, I(rD), \qquad 0 < r < 1.
\end{equation}
Both populations share the same DOS and the same shape of $D(V)$, so the split costs two global parameters and no per-scan freedom, and must therefore hold at every sweep rate at once. Physically it corresponds to the ordered and disordered regions of a semicrystalline film, which take up solvated ions at very different rates. Since almost all of the evidence separating the two environments lives in the sweep-rate dependence, we refuse the split below three scan rates and fall back to a single diffusivity.

The total number of particles $N(t)$ in the film is found by integrating the solution $u(x,t)$ over the spatial domain $[0,L]$. The pure Faradaic current is then given by $I = \frac{dN}{dt}$, evaluated numerically as a finite difference:
\begin{equation}
    I_{\text{sim}}(t) = \frac{\Delta N}{\Delta t}
\end{equation}
No other term is added, and nothing is fitted per scan. An earlier version carried a constant current offset for each scan; removing it changed the fits by less than 0.05 percentage points.
Earlier versions also carried background exponential tails imitating Tafel kinetics at the ends of the sweep. These have been removed. The fitted decay constant converged onto whichever bound it was given rather than onto a value fixed by the data, which is the mark of a general-purpose smoother; and because the tails carried per-scan freedom, they hid a genuine disagreement between scan rates, which is exactly what fitting the scans jointly exists to expose.

\subsection{Series Resistance}

Some films show a peak separation that grows with the current, with sharp cut-offs on one side of a peak; ECP-Magenta is the clearest case. This is the signature of an uncompensated resistance $R$ in the cell. The film then does not see the applied potential, but
\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{film}}(t) = V(t) - R\,I(t),
\end{equation}
where $I$ is the film's own current. Inserting the measured current here is ill-conditioned: wherever the measured current changes quickly, $V_{\text{film}}$ jumps and the simulated current spikes. We therefore solve the condition self-consistently. Over one time step the recursion for the $T_m$ makes the number of ions in the film affine in the new boundary value,
\begin{equation}
    N(t_i) = a_i\, f\!\left(V_{\text{film}}(t_i)\right) + b_i, \qquad 0 < a_i \le L,
\end{equation}
since $a_i = L - \sum_m c_m \lambda_m^{-1} \frac{1 - e^{-D\lambda_m^2\Delta t_i}}{D\lambda_m^2\Delta t_i}$ and $\sum_m c_m\lambda_m^{-1} = L$. The condition
\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{film}} - V(t_i) + R\,\frac{N(t_i) - N(t_{i-1})}{\Delta t_i} = 0
\end{equation}
is then increasing in $V_{\text{film}}$ and has exactly one root, which a few Newton steps find. Only the last Newton step is differentiated, which by the implicit function theorem gives the exact gradient at the root. The periodic initial condition comes from Eq.~\ref{Init Time} applied to the previous pass over the cycle, starting from $V - R\,I_{\text{exp}}$, and two passes converge it. Where the data points are more than 10 mV apart the steps are refined to 10 mV.

With $R = 0$ the model is exactly the one above. On ECP-Magenta, fitting $R$ gives about 89 $\Omega$ and lowers the residual from 5.4\% to 2.0\%. The resistance is well determined; the shape of $D(V)$ is not, since both exponents and $V_c$ end on their bounds.

\subsection{Optimization Strategy}

To extract the physical parameters, we frame the task as an optimization problem. Every supplied scan rate is fitted at the same time against one shared $D(V)$ and one shared DOS; this is what it means to treat $D(V)$ and the DOS as properties of the film. The objective is the mean squared error between the simulated current $I_{\text{sim}}$ and the experimental current $I_{\text{exp}}$, each scan divided by its own measured range so that no scan dominates the fit simply by carrying more current:
\begin{equation}
    \mathcal{L} = \frac{1}{S}\sum_{s=1}^{S} \frac{\left\langle \left(I_{\text{sim}} - I_{\text{exp}}\right)^2 \right\rangle_s}{\left(\max I_{\text{exp}} - \min I_{\text{exp}}\right)_s^2}
\end{equation}
Earlier versions weighted each point by the curvature of the smoothed experimental current, and added a second-difference smoothing penalty on the weights $w_p$. We tested both on three films (the 0.1~g/L film, ProDOT and ECP-Magenta), and neither was needed. Without the weighting, the plain residual came out slightly lower on all three. Without the penalty, the error on a held-out scan was the same or lower, and the DOS was just as reproducible from fit to fit with one scan left out each time. The penalty had also flattened the narrow peaks of ECP-Magenta. The potential window must still match the data: sub-bands centred well outside it are full or empty for the whole sweep and carry no current, so the data cannot set their weights, and a window reaching more than 50 mV past the data is clipped to it.

We minimize the loss using the L-BFGS-B algorithm, with exact gradients computed by JAX's reverse-mode automatic differentiation (\texttt{jax.value\_and\_grad}). The diffusivity enters as $\log_{10} D_0$, since a film at equilibrium sends it up by decades. The fit starts from a DOS read off the slowest scan: near equilibrium the current divided by the sweep rate is the DOS itself, so half the gap between the two sweeps, divided by the sub-band spacing, gives the starting weights. A flat start does not work; on ECP-Magenta it stalls at a poor minimum. From this start the optimization runs in at most two stages:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Film:} every parameter except $R$ is fitted at once. Earlier versions opened the parameters up in warm-up stages (baseline, weights, then diffusivity); on ProDOT and ECP-Magenta those led to a worse minimum, so they were dropped.
    \item \textbf{Series Resistance}, only when it is switched on: all parameters, now including $R$, are refitted with the film driven at $V - RI$. Released from the start, $R$ can run to its bound, which is why it comes last.
\end{enumerate}

Upon convergence, this routine yields the voltage-dependent diffusion $D(V)$ and the Density of States for the experimental chemical system.
